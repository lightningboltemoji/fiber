The quiet return of the night train

The sleeper carriage is back: slower than a flight, stranger than a hotel, and somehow exactly what we wanted. A journey from Lisbon to Madrid, in the dark.

By Clara Valadares·Illustrations by Anders Reiff·October 4, 2026
Listen to this storyRead by the author · 31 min

At twenty to ten on a Thursday night, the departures board at Santa Apolónia shows a line that was not there five years ago. Between the last commuter train to Azambuja and a delayed regional to Tomar, in the same unhurried white letters, it reads: Atlântico Nocturno — Madrid Chamartín — 21:40. Nobody runs for it. That is the first thing you notice about the people who take the night train. They arrive early, they carry too much bread, and they wear the faintly conspiratorial look of people who know something the airport doesn’t.

I had come to find out what that something was. For most of my adult life the overnight train has been a ghost: a thing my grandparents did, a scene in a black-and-white film, a timetable entry deleted quietly in the name of efficiency. The last regular sleeper between Lisbon and Madrid stopped in the spring of 2020, and for a long time it looked as if it would never start again. And yet here it is, eleven carriages long and painted the deep green of a wine bottle, humming at platform three with its blinds half-drawn, like a house where somebody is still awake.

Part One

A hotel that moves

The conductor’s name is Rui Sousa, and he carries a brass key on a lanyard that he has, he tells me, never once used. “It opens the old doors,” he says, nodding at the carriage behind him, a sleeper from the 1980s that spent nine years in a siding outside Entroncamento before a Lisbon cooperative bought it for the price of a small flat. “These ones are electric now. But the key is tradition.” He checks my ticket by hand, writes my cabin number on a card in pencil, and hands it back with the gravity of a hotel concierge.

That is not an accident. The people behind the new night trains talk about them less as transport than as hospitality. “Nobody wants to be transported,” says Mariana Leal, who runs operations for the cooperative and who, at thirty-four, is younger than most of its rolling stock. “A plane transports you. A night train looks after you. You go to sleep in one city and you wake up in another, and in between someone has brought you tea.”

My cabin is at the far end of carriage seven: two berths, one folded up against the wall like the page of a book, a washbasin hidden beneath a walnut lid, a single carnation in a clip by the window. There is a bottle of water, a small towel embroidered with a crescent moon, and a printed card explaining, in four languages, how to lock the door. It is roughly the size of a wardrobe. I love it immediately.

You do not travel on a night train. You are delivered by one, the way a letter is.

At 21:40 exactly, without a whistle or an announcement, the platform begins to slide away. Lisbon goes past in slices: the dark dome of the Panteão, the cranes along the river, a terrace bar where someone is laughing. Then the river itself, wide and black and full of lights, as the train turns north along the Tagus and settles into the long, low murmur it will keep up all night.

To understand why the night train disappeared, you have to understand how badly it fits a spreadsheet. A sleeper carriage carries perhaps thirty people where a day coach carries eighty. It runs once a day, in one direction, and spends the daylight hours parked and earning nothing. It needs staff who stay awake, linen that must be washed, and track that freight operators would rather use at night. When the budget airlines arrived, offering the same journey in an hour for the price of a sandwich, the arithmetic was brutal.

“Every night route in Europe was losing money, and every one of them was losing it in a slightly different currency,” says Henrique Abreu, a transport economist at the Institute for Mobility Studies in Porto, who has spent fifteen years tracking the decline and return of overnight rail. “Then three things changed at once. Carbon got a price. Airports got miserable. And a generation of travellers decided that the journey was allowed to be part of the trip.”

The numbers he shows me are startling. In 2015 there were fewer than twenty regular overnight routes left in western Europe. This winter there will be sixty-three, run by a patchwork of state railways, private start-ups and at least two cooperatives, one of which began as a crowdfunding campaign with a hand-drawn logo. Ridership on the revived lines has grown by roughly a third every year since 2022. On the busiest routes, cabins for the summer sell out by March.

Not all of it is good news. Many of the new services run on second-hand carriages bought wherever they can be found: from Germany, from Austria, and in one case a single stray coach from a defunct Scandinavian line, still fitted with its original reading lamps. Track access charges vary wildly at every border. And the trains are slow, not romantically slow but slow in ways that could be fixed and haven’t been. “The night train is not a museum piece,” Abreu says. “It’s a piece of infrastructure we stopped maintaining. That’s a different problem.”

Part Three

Inside the sleeper carriage

By eleven, the corridor has turned into a kind of street. Doors stand open. A Dutch couple in matching fleeces are sharing a bottle of vinho verde with a retired surveyor from Évora. Two students from Salamanca are playing cards on the floor, using a folded timetable as a table. A small boy in dinosaur pyjamas walks the length of the carriage twice, gravely, as though inspecting it, and is then carried off to bed.

The sleeper carriage is one of the great small machines of the twentieth century, and almost nothing about it has changed. Each cabin is a puzzle box: the seat that becomes the lower berth, the upper berth that drops on chains, the ladder that hooks onto a rail, the washbasin that folds away to reveal a shelf. Everything has two uses and a place to go. “It’s like sleeping inside a Swiss army knife,” says one of the students, without looking up from her cards.

The carriages on the Atlântico Nocturno have been modernised with a light hand: a socket by each pillow, a better mattress, a key card in place of Rui’s brass key. But the bones are old, and the engineers who restored them insisted on keeping the things passengers remember. The little blue night light above the door. The wooden handrail worn pale at the height of a hand. The window blind with its leather tab, which you can pull down for privacy or leave open a crack, so you can watch the dark go past.

There is a ritual to going to bed in a moving room, and everyone seems to learn it within the hour. Shoes go in the net. Glasses go in the little hammock by the pillow. You brush your teeth at the walnut basin with the bottled water, swaying slightly, like a sailor. Then you climb the ladder, switch off the main light, and lie there in the blue glow, listening to the wheels, feeling the country go by underneath you.

Part Four

Lisbon to Madrid, the long way round

There is a faster way to make this journey, and everyone on the train knows it. The flight takes an hour and fifteen minutes. A high-speed line, half-built and much delayed, will one day bring Lisbon to Madrid inside five hours. The Atlântico Nocturno takes ten hours and forty-two minutes, and it takes them on purpose, crawling east across the Alentejo and climbing slowly into the hills of the frontier while almost everyone aboard is asleep.

I am not asleep. At half past midnight I am standing in the corridor with the window down, watching the last lights of Portugal disappear. Near Marvão the line climbs through cork oak and granite, and the train slows almost to walking pace. A fox looks up from the trackside, unbothered. The border, when it comes, is nothing: no barrier, no announcement, only a change in the shape of the station signs and the moment when my phone, after some thought, decides that it is an hour later than it was.

The dining car stays open until two. It is the most beautiful room I have been in this year: twelve tables under brass lamps, white cloths, a narrow bar where the steward, Inmaculada, makes a hot chocolate so thick the spoon stands up in it. A man in a linen suit is writing letters by hand. A woman across the aisle tells me she has taken this train every month for a year, ever since her daughter moved to Madrid. “I could fly,” she says. “But then I would arrive tired and sad. This way, I arrive.”

A flight is a gap in your life. The night train fills that gap with something.

Somewhere past Cáceres I give up and go to bed. The berth is narrower than any bed I have slept in as an adult, and I sleep better than I have in months. The train has a rhythm, a soft double knock every few seconds, the long sigh of the brakes at a signal, the faint rocking on the curves, that seems to have been designed by someone who understood exactly how tired people are.

Part Five

Morning, Chamartín

I wake just after eight to light the colour of weak tea coming in around the edge of the blind. Outside, the Castilian plateau is rolling past, enormous and empty and gold, and storks stand on their huge untidy nests on top of the electricity pylons. Rui knocks with coffee and a pastry in a paper bag. He has been awake all night, he says, and it has been a quiet one: one lost passport, quickly found, and a gentleman who wanted to know whether the train could slow down so he could finish his book.

We arrive at Madrid Chamartín at 09:22, on time to the minute. The platform is full of commuters in a hurry, and for a moment the passengers stepping off the night train look like visitors from somewhere else: rumpled, unhurried, blinking in the light, carrying the last of the bread. The Dutch couple wave. The boy in dinosaur pyjamas is now dressed and appears to be in charge of the family’s luggage.

Henrique Abreu had told me, back in Porto, that the night train would never again be the main way Europeans travel, and that this was fine. “It doesn’t need to win,” he said. “It needs to exist. It needs to be one of the choices.” Standing on the platform at Chamartín, I think he is right, and also that he has slightly undersold it. A flight is a gap in your life. The night train fills that gap with something: a conversation, a hot chocolate, a fox in the dark, a few hours of the deepest sleep you have had all year. It gives you back the night.
