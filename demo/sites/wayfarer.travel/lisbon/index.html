48 Hours in Lisbon

Seven hills, one yellow tram and a pastry case on every corner: how to spend a perfect weekend in Europe’s sunniest capital, from the first bica to the last fado.

Lisbon rewards the unhurried. The city spills down seven hills to the wide, glittering mouth of the Tagus, and nearly every street seems to end at a view: a tiled church, a sliver of river, a line of laundry strung between two balconies. You could spend a month here and still be surprised by a staircase. You have two days. Here’s how to spend them.

Pack shoes with grip, because the limestone calçada underfoot has been polished to a shine by three centuries of footsteps, and leave room in your bag for tins of sardines. Everything else you’ll find on the way.

01

Day one

Alfama, Baixa & Chiado

08:30

A bica and a nata in Graça

Start where the locals do: standing at a zinc counter. Café Andorinha, a narrow room of mirrors and swallow-patterned tiles below the Graça church, has been pulling short, syrupy espressos (a bica, in Lisbon) since 1951. Order yours with a pastel de nata still warm from the oven, and dust it with cinnamon from the shaker on the counter.

Baking at home? Saltwater Kitchen’s pastéis de nata are the closest we’ve found to the real thing.

10:00

Ride the 28 tram through Alfama

The 28 tram is a rattling wooden time machine, all brass handles and varnished benches, and its route is the best tour of the old city money can buy. Board at Graça rather than the crowded terminus at Martim Moniz, take a seat on the right, and ride it down through Alfama’s knotted lanes, close enough to the walls to touch the geraniums on the windowsills.

Hop off at Largo das Portas do Sol and walk the rest. Alfama is best explored by getting lost: follow the sound of a radio, a staircase, a cat.

12:30

Grilled sardines at Taberna da Rua Torta

Lunch is a plate of sardines, blistered over charcoal and laid on a thick slice of bread that soaks up the juices, with a jug of cold vinho verde. Taberna da Rua Torta has six tables, a paper tablecloth and a grandmother at the grill. Arrive before one o’clock or expect to wait, happily, on the steps outside.

15:00

Baixa’s grid and Chiado’s bookshops

After the great earthquake of 1755 levelled the lower town, it was rebuilt on a strict, rational grid, and the Baixa still feels like a stage set: arcades, pale yellow façades, streets named for the trades that once filled them. Walk up Rua Augusta to the triumphal arch, then climb into Chiado, where Livraria Sete Colinas sells secondhand Pessoa in four languages and café tables spill onto the square.

18:45

Sunset at Senhora do Monte

Lisbon has dozens of miradouros, or viewpoints, and every local has a favourite. Ours is the Miradouro da Senhora do Monte, the highest in the city, where an umbrella pine frames the castle and the whole of the lower town glows copper as the sun drops behind the bridge. Bring a bottle; everyone else will.

21:00

Fado, somewhere small

Fado is Lisbon’s song of longing, and it’s best heard in a room that seats thirty. At Casa do Silêncio the lights drop, the twelve-string guitarra begins, and nobody says a word until the last note. Dinner is simple: caldo verde, salt cod, a glass of something red from the Alentejo. The music is not.

02

Day two

Belém, the river & Príncipe Real

09:00

West along the river to Belém

Take the 15 tram west along the waterfront to Belém, where the age of exploration left behind its grandest monuments. The Jerónimos Monastery’s cloister, its stone carved as finely as lace, is worth the early start: arrive at opening and you’ll have it nearly to yourself.

11:30

Custard tarts, round two

Belém is the spiritual home of the pastel de nata, and the queue at its most famous bakery is legendary. Skip it. Confeitaria Lume, two streets back, bakes in small batches all morning, with darker, more caramelised tops and pastry that shatters the way it should.

13:30

Seafood on the Cais do Sodré

Back in town, Cervejaria Maré does everything you want from a Lisbon seafood hall: clams in garlic and coriander, prawns by the kilo, a steak sandwich to finish. It’s loud, bright and entirely wonderful.

16:00

The cedar of Príncipe Real

Spend the afternoon in Príncipe Real, the city’s most elegant neighbourhood, browsing ceramics and linen in converted palaces. In the garden at its centre, a single cedar has been trained over a wide iron frame into a living parasol; sit beneath it with an ice cream and watch the old men play cards.

18:30

Cross the river at golden hour

End where Lisbon makes the most sense: from the water. The orange ferry to Cacilhas leaves the Cais do Sodré every twenty minutes, costs less than a bica and a nata, and takes ten minutes to cross. Stand at the stern. As the boat pulls away the city rises behind you in tiers of terracotta and white, lit gold, with the castle on top like a crown.

Have dinner on the far bank, then catch the last boat home.

    Where to stay

    Three bases for a long weekend, at three very different prices.

    Alfama

    Casa Azulejo

    Nine rooms behind a tiled eighteenth-century façade, with breakfast on the roof terrace and the cathedral bells for an alarm clock.

    From €185 a night

    Príncipe Real

    The Miradouro

    A former printworks with a rooftop pool, a natural-wine bar and the best sunset view of any hotel in the city.

    From €260 a night

    Baixa

    Pensão Laranjeira

    A family-run guesthouse arranged around a courtyard of lemon trees. Simple, spotless and steps from everything.

    From €110 a night

    Know before you go

    The practical bits, so the rest of the weekend can be anything but.

    Getting there

    The airport is twenty minutes from the centre by metro. Coming from Spain? The overnight sleeper from Madrid pulls into Santa Apolónia just after breakfast.

    Getting around

    Buy a rechargeable transit card at any metro station. It covers trams, buses, the funiculars and the river ferries.

    When to go

    May, June and September bring long, warm days without the August crowds. Winters are mild, and the light is lovely.
